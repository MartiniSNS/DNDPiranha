Magic is an old thing, braided into the land in places and rarer in others. In a moonlit glade you can sense it like a humming beneath the roots; in a crowded market it is the thing people whisper about, half fear and half wonder. Some spellcraft is loud and showy; most of it is quiet work, charms stitched into shoes, warded letters slipped under doors, small acts that keep a well from freezing or a barn from burning. 
Magic is a tool, a terror, a song, and a stigma. It can open doors and close them; it can be the reason a child smiles or the reason a lord signs your name into a ledger of suspicion. Play it like that: sometimes loud, sometimes secret, and always with the knowledge that the world’s memory of magic will shape the welcome you get, for better, and for worse.

For some background: Two hundred years ago magic ran like rivers across the land, apprentices crowded guild towers, hedge‑mages kept blights at bay, and ley lines thrummed beneath market squares, until a cabal of necromancers sought to pry open doors no mortal should touch. They chanted to the void, tore at the seams between worlds, and in their arrogance rent a wound in the fabric of time and space: rifts that spat nightmares, storms of cold light, and creatures that should not be. Farmers and archers, clerics and wizards, lords and commoners alike rose to close that wound; in a terrible, desperate campaign they fought across ravaged fields and shattered wards, binding the rift with spells and bloody steel. They succeeded, but the price was counted in graves, entire circles of mages used themselves as anchors, cities bled men and women, and whole stretches of the land were left aether‑poor. Since then the world remembers both the ruin and the rescue: raw magic never recovered its old abundance, many arcane wells ran dry or were sealed by wary councils, and those who still weave spells do so quietly, for admiration and fear now walk hand in hand. Hidden pockets of power linger where the weave is thin, but most folk treat a mage as you might treat a tinderbox near a dry barn, useful, dangerous, and kept at a wary distance
